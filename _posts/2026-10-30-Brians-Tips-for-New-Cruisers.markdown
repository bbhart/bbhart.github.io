This generally assumes you're a first time cruiser but more experienced folks might still find some nuggets here. This is informed by my own experiences; as of this writing I've sailed 19 times (138 nights) on Disney, Celebrity, Royal Caribbean, and Silversea -- not including the ~8 months I lived and worked aboard the Disney Wonder early in my career.

## Finding Your First Cruise

You've never sailed before. You're perhaps nervous about getting sea sick, getting regular sick, not having enough to do, not liking your cabin, not liking the ports, not knowing how to get to the ship, and on and on. There's a lot to think about, with no frame of reference to draw from. 

As you're planning your **first** cruise, here are some things to keep in mind. After this, read the Finding a Cruise section.

### Cruise duration
A 3 or 4 night cruise out of Port Canaveral or Galveston attracts a different crowd than a 12 night Mediterranean out of Barcelona. If you're nervous about seasickness, you might decide to book a a 3 night cruise with the intention of limiting your time onboard if you're feeling ill or if there's too much of a party atmosphere or other vibes problems. The challenge here, though, is that a 7 night cruise isn't just a 3 night cruise but longer. It's a different mood. On a 7 night, there's more time to relax, settle in, get to know the staff, establish routines, make "ship friends", have opinions about favorite restaurants and dishes, etc. Unless my client had strong opinions one way or the other, I would recommend a 7 night as a first cruise.

### Cruise line
Your first cruise will probably be on one of these lines, mainly because they are the heavy hitters in the industry with the most inventory:

Mainstream: Carnival Cruise Line, MSC Cruises, Norwegian Cruise Line, Royal Caribbean
Premium: Celebrity Cruises, Holland America, Princess Cruises

There are other tiers like Luxury, Expedition, and River, but let's stick to Mainstream and Premium for now. 

Within each _line_, there are different _classes_ of ships. Let's use Celebrity as an example, since I'm most familiar with them. Celebrity has three main _ocean_ ship classes:

**Edge class** (newest and largest): Celebrity Edge, Celebrity Apex, Celebrity Beyond, Celebrity Ascent and Celebrity Xcel.
**Solstice class:** Celebrity Solstice, Celebrity Equinox, Celebrity Eclipse, Celebrity Silhouette and Celebrity Reflection.
**Millennium class** (oldest and smallest): Celebrity Millennium, Celebrity Infinity, Celebrity Summit and Celebrity Constellation. All four were refitted ("Revolutionized") in recent years.

There will be smaller variations between ships in the same _class_ and then larger variations between the different _classes_. If you sail on the Solstice and then go on the Eclipse (same class), it will feel familiar but with subtle differences. If you sail on the Eclipse and then sail on the Apex (different class, same company), you'll see meaningful differences everywhere -- number and type of restaurants, pool locations, elevator locations, public spaces -- but there will still (mostly) be the level of service you received on the other ship. 

I could go on and on here. The takeaway here is the ship class you choose matters. In my personal experience in 2026, I received better overall service on the Icon class of Royal Caribbean (a mainstream line) than on the Edge class of Celebrity (a premium line). This is where you want to lean on your travel advisor!

### Ship Size

If you follow the guidance above and hew toward a mainstream or premium line for your first cruise, you'll have _some_ variability in the size of the cruise ship, but will still likely be with at least 2000 fellow passengers. The largest Royal Caribbean ships carry more than 5600 people! The largest ships almost defy description and are part floating hotel, part theme park, part water park, part shopping mall. 

With the industry trend towards larger and larger ships, those large ships also tend to be the newest, which may influence your decision. Larger (heavier) ships tend to be less rocky on the seas, if that's a concern -- but you can never truly do away with ship rocking, so I wouldn't let that be _the_ factor in choosing a ship size. 

At the other end of the spectrum from the Royal Caribbean mega-ships, once you get into ultra-luxury lines like Silversea of Seabourn, you can find ships with as few as 100 guests, with the mid-200's being more common!

## Cabin location

I'll go deeper on cabin location in the next section, but for your first cruise I'd recommend booking a stateroom mid-ship. This part of the ship is the most stable, if you're not sure how your sea legs will be.

### Where Do You Want To Go?

Generally, the regions are: Alaska, Antarctica, Asia, Australia and New Zealand, Bermuda, Canada and New England, Caribbean, Hawaii, Mediterranean, Mexican Riviera, Northern Europe, Panama Canal, South America, South Pacific, Transatlantic. 

Some of this may be decided for you based on how much time you have off work and how far you want to fly. Seasonality also matters. Alaska itineraries run May through September when it's warmer in the northern hemisphere, while Antarctica is accessible November through March.
